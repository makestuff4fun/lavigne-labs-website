Most people who source in China know Alibaba.com. Some know 1688. Far fewer think of Taobao, which looks like a consumer shopping site for phone cases and sneakers. I'd argue it's worth a look anyway, because a lot of small factories and workshops advertise there. Believe it or not, it is a cheap way to put a business in front of customers.

## Why factories are on Taobao

A shop on Taobao is cheap to run. There is no trade-show booth, no English-speaking sales team, no polished export catalogue. A small workshop with a few machines can post photos of what it makes, set a price, and let buyers message it. That is enough.

The result is a hidden business-to-business ecosystem sitting in plain sight, mixed in with the consumer listings. Things you can find there include:

- **Raw-material suppliers**: sheet, bar and extrusion, often cut to length.
- **Custom heatsinks**: extruded or machined to your drawing.
- **Laser cutting and bending**: send a file, get flat or formed parts back.
- **Custom wire harnesses and cables**: made to your length, connector and pinout.
- **Connectors**: from common catalogue parts to less common ones.
- **Other custom processes**: the list is long, and it changes.

I can't tell you how large this ecosystem is, and I won't invent a number. What I can say is that it is there, and most foreign buyers never look.

## How the three platforms differ

**Alibaba.com** is export-facing. The listings are in English, the sales reps speak English, and that is the convenience. It is also the cost. This is where Brian's Law applies:

> **"If the factory can deal with you in English, you can find a lower price."**

That is Brian's rule of thumb, not mine, and it is a rule of thumb rather than a physical law. But it explains why the English-friendly platform is usually not the cheapest one.

**1688** is Alibaba's domestic wholesale site. It is Chinese-language and aimed at buyers who order in volume, so it often has minimum order quantities that don't suit a prototype run.

**Taobao** is built for individual buyers, so many shops will sell one piece. For a sample or a small first order, that matters a great deal.

## How to search

Search in Chinese, and search by process, not by product. A few terms that are a good starting point:

- **激光切割** (jīguāng qiēgē): laser cutting
- **折弯** (zhéwān): bending, usually sheet-metal bending
- **散热器定制** (sànrèqì dìngzhì): custom heatsinks
- **线束加工** (xiànshù jiāgōng): wire harness processing or assembly
- **连接器** (liánjiēqì): connectors
- **型材** (xíngcái): profiles, meaning extrusions
- **板材** (bǎncái): sheet or plate material

Add the material or a dimension and see what comes up. Combine terms too: laser cutting plus bending is often sold together. If you don't read Chinese, a translation tool is enough to get started, but check any technical term against a native speaker before you order.

## Read the listing like a quote

Many process listings are priced per piece, or per sample, with options for material, thickness or length. Treat that as a starting point, not a final quote. Look for photos of real work rather than stock images, and whether the shop shows machines, not only finished parts. Reviews help, but they are mostly from other small buyers, so read them with that in mind.

## Get behind the shop

The shop is often just the front door. Taobao buyers talk to sellers through **旺旺** (Aliwangwang), the built-in chat. Message the shop, send your drawing, and ask direct questions: do you make this yourselves, what tolerance can you hold, can you quote a small batch. A friendly answer from the person at the machines is often how you discover that there is a real factory behind the listing, and that it will quote you a proper price outside the shop page.

The same advice I'd give for any supplier applies here. Ask to see the process, ask for photos or video of your kind of work, and don't take "no problem" as an answer to every requirement. (See the article on vetting a factory.)

## Order samples cheaply

This is the real advantage. If shops sell by the piece, a sample costs very little compared with a trip, or a minimum order on another platform. Order a few, measure them, test the fit, and compare shops before you commit to anything larger. Run the same drawing through two or three shops and you learn quickly who holds tolerances and who doesn't.

## The limits

I'd be doing you a disservice if I stopped there. This route has real friction:

- **You need Chinese.** Listings, chat and often the drawings' notes are in Chinese. Misunderstanding a spec is easy.
- **Accounts and payment.** Taobao is built for buyers inside China. Whether a foreign buyer can open an account and pay with a foreign card has changed over time. Currently, check before relying on it. Many foreign buyers end up using a local contact to place the order.
- **Shipping out of China.** Many shops ship only within China, and some won't ship abroad at all. Check whether you need a forwarder, and what customs treatment you will face. Don't assume it works the way it does on an export platform.
- **Quality varies.** A low-cost shop can be excellent or poor, and the listing won't tell you which. Inspect samples before you scale up.
- **Not everything fits.** Anything safety-critical, certified or tightly toleranced deserves a proper factory vetting, not a chat window.

## Is it worth it?

For prototypes, small runs, and standard-ish custom parts like cut and bent sheet, heatsinks and cables, I think so. It lets you learn what things cost when the seller isn't pricing in an export sales team, which is exactly what Brian's Law predicts. For larger programs, treat Taobao as a way to discover the right kind of workshop, then move to a proper supplier relationship.
