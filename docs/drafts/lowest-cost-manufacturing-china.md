When a client asks me how to get the lowest price out of China, I usually start with a rule I've come to rely on. I call it Brian's Law:

> **"If the factory can deal with you in English, you can find a lower price."**

It's a rule of thumb, not a physical law. There are exceptions, and I'll get to them. But in my experience it holds often enough that I check it on almost every project.

## Why the Law works

Think about what it takes for a factory to talk to a foreign buyer comfortably. Someone has to speak fluent English, answer email in your time zone, send polished quotes, and often travel to trade shows. That is a sales team, and a sales team is overhead. Somebody pays for it, and it's the buyer, built into the price.

Often the English-speaking company isn't the maker at all. It's a trading company, or a factory that has grown a trading arm, sitting between you and the people running the machines. Each layer can add its own margin. I've seen jobs pass through more than one before they reach the shop floor.

None of that is dishonest. Those companies do real work: they simplify communication and take on some risk. But if your goal is the lowest cost, you are paying for convenience, and the real maker is often one or two tiers further down, in a smaller factory that doesn't have an English-speaking front office.

## Big factories quietly outsource

Even when you've found a real factory with real machines, your order may not be made there. Larger factories often subcontract work to smaller shops. They do it when they are at capacity, when a process isn't their strength (plating, certain machining, tooling, finishing), or when a smaller shop can simply do the job cheaper.

Signs that it may be happening:

- **The quote is fast for a process the line doesn't show.** If they can't show you the machine that makes your part, someone else probably does.
- **Different parts, different lead times that don't fit one building.** A part arriving from outside for plating or secondary operations is normal. Your whole part arriving from outside is a different matter.
- **Vague answers about who does what.** Good factories are usually proud of their process and happy to explain it.
- **Unmarked or oddly packed parts at the dock**, or parts that show up with a different finish or tool marks than the sample.
- **A refusal to let you see the process step by step.** Not always a red flag, but worth asking about.

This is why the first rule of my factory vetting is to see the line that makes your kind of product. If the big factory is subcontracting, you want to know, and often you'll want to go meet the subcontractor.

## Small factories: the upside and the risk

The smaller shops one or two tiers down often have only a handful of clients, and they depend heavily on them. That cuts both ways.

**The upside**

- **Price.** Less overhead, no sales team, often lower margins accepted to keep the machines busy.
- **Attention.** A new client can matter a lot to a small shop. The owner may answer the phone.
- **Flexibility.** Fewer layers means faster decisions: a change to a drawing or a small batch can happen without a committee.

**The risk**

- **Financial fragility.** A small factory with thin cash reserves may struggle if a customer pays late or a material price jumps.
- **A client loss can sink them.** If one large client is most of the business and leaves, the whole shop is in trouble, and so is your order.
- **Capacity gets squeezed.** When the big client calls with a rush, your job can be bumped. You're the small customer now, and the shop's loyalty goes to whoever pays the bills.

Neither side is automatically better. A dependent small factory can be an excellent partner or a ticking clock, and you only find out by looking closely.

## How to find and vet them

The honest answer is that this is harder than it sounds, and it's why most buyers stop at the English-speaking layer.

- **1688.** The domestic Chinese wholesale and sourcing platform, 1688, lists makers in Chinese, at prices aimed at domestic buyers. It's not a sourcing strategy by itself, but it shows you who exists and roughly what they charge.
- **Industrial clusters.** Many product types are concentrated in particular regions, with whole supply chains within a short drive. Going to the cluster beats searching from far away.
- **Visit in person.** I won't recommend a small factory I haven't walked. Look at the machines, the QC, the floor, who's actually working, and how much work is in the shop from other clients.
- **Use a Chinese-speaking agent or engineer.** The people who run these shops often don't work in English, and the real conversation about tolerances, materials and process happens in Chinese. That is what we do at Lavigne Labs: we speak with the factory directly, on site, and make sure what you meant is what they heard.

Everything in my vetting checklist still applies: capability against your real tolerances, capacity during your window, a reference sample everyone agrees on. A smaller factory just needs it more, because there is less margin for error.

## When not to chase the lowest price

The cheapest quote is not always the lowest cost. I'd think twice when:

- **The product is safety-critical or regulated**, and a failure costs far more than the savings.
- **The volumes are small.** Savings per unit may not justify the extra travel, vetting, and management time.
- **The design isn't stable.** Frequent changes need a responsive partner, and sometimes that is the English-speaking one.
- **Your timeline is tight.** Finding and qualifying a new shop takes time.
- **You need the factory to be stable.** If you can't tolerate the risk of a fragile supplier, paying for a bigger, steadier one is a fair trade.
- **You can't have someone on the ground.** A lower price without oversight can turn into rework and delay.

Also remember that unit price is only part of the story. See my piece on what landed cost includes before comparing quotes.

## A short checklist

1. Can the factory deal with you easily in English? If yes, ask who actually makes the part.
2. Ask to see the line that makes your product, running.
3. Ask which processes are done in-house and which are subcontracted.
4. Look for the smaller shop one or two tiers down: 1688, the local cluster, referrals.
5. Visit in person, or send someone who speaks the language.
6. Ask honestly how many clients they have and how much one client matters.
7. Check capacity during your production window.
8. Agree on a reference sample before the run.
9. Decide whether the savings are worth the risk for this product.

## Closing thought

Brian's Law isn't a promise of a cheaper price. It's a prompt to keep digging when everything is too easy. Sometimes the easy path is right, but you should choose it knowingly, not because it's the only one you could see.
